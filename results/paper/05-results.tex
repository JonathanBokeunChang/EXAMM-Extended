Prior work employed two constructions~\cite{lyu2025evostar}. The \emph{individual} construction fits a separate model per stock, so no information is shared across the universe. The \emph{concatenated} construction fits one model over all stocks by stacking their predictors into a wide series (300 inputs, 50 outputs), making input dimensionality scale with universe size. The \emph{pooled panel} adopted here retains the six-predictor, single-output structure of the individual construction while fitting one model across all stocks, so parameters are shared across the universe and input dimensionality remains fixed regardless of how many stocks are included.

Table~\ref{tab:pooling-ablation} compares pooled and individual training on the Baseline Portfolio, reporting test-period IC, MSE, and the return of a simple long/short portfolio that holds the ten highest-ranked and shorts the ten lowest-ranked stocks. Pooling improves every metric in both the 2022 and 2023 folds. The gain in MSE is modest, however the effect on cross-sectional ranking ability is substantially larger: individual training produces near-zero IC in 2022 and negative IC in 2023, meaning its predictions carry little or no usable signal about relative performance, whereas pooled training attains positive IC in both folds. Because the long/short strategy allocates on relative rather than absolute predictions, this difference in ranking quality, rather than the small MSE gap, drives the trading results. Pooling improves the long/short return in 2022 and, in 2023, converts a strategy that loses money under individual training into a profitable one. We therefore adopt pooled training for all subsequent experiments.

\subsection{EXAMM Hyperparameter Search}
\label{sec:examm-hparams}

Speciation is central to convergence behaviour in neuroevolutionary search: the island
population structure determines how much architectural diversity is maintained, while the
repopulation interval controls how aggressively converged islands are reseeded from elsewhere in
the population. Too frequent repopulation discards partially converged architectures before they
can be refined; too infrequent repopulation allows islands to stagnate. We therefore swept both
parameters jointly, over island counts $\{10, 20, 40\}$ and repopulation intervals
$\{250, 500, 1000\}$, evaluating all $9$ configurations on both test cohorts.

Table~\ref{tab:hp-grid} reports the results. The repopulation interval is the more consequential
of the two parameters: the $500$ setting yields the strongest returns at every island count in
both cohorts, whereas $250$ and $1000$ are inconsistent and in several configurations produce
losses. Island settings differ across 2022 and 2023 test year.
Given that additional islands increase search cost without a corresponding gain, we adopt
$(10~\text{islands}, 500)$ repopulation frequency for all subsequent experiments.

\subsection{EXAMM vs Transformers}
\label{sec:examm-vs-transformers}
Transformer models generally perform well on real-world, multidimensional data, yet simpler architectures such as linear models have been claimed to outperform them for time series forecasting~\cite{zeng2023transformers}. We bring these different families of models together to compare their ability to forecast stock returns and how that translates into portfolio returns.

We also compared EXAMM against state-of-the-art time series forecasting models, both Transformer-based and otherwise, in their ability to forecast stock returns and how those predictions translate into portfolio investment returns. We tested the linear model DLinear~\cite{zeng2023transformers}, along with the Transformer-based PatchTST~\cite{nie2022time}, iTransformer~\cite{liu2024itransformer}, DeformTime~\cite{shu2024deformtime}, and Crossformer~\cite{zhang2023crossformer}, with an input length of $96$ and an output length of $1$. We chose an input length of $96$ because it is the most widely reported input sequence length across these models, and an output length of $1$ so that the results are directly comparable to the one-step-ahead forecasts of the EXAMM-evolved RNNs. All other hyperparameters were adopted from the default settings.

Tables~\ref{tab:forecasting} and~\ref{tab:portfolio} separate the two claims
that the single combined table conflated. Forecasting accuracy is reported
first, on its own terms; portfolio performance follows, together with the
turnover that connects them.

The connection is worth stating before the numbers, because the two tables
disagree if it is left implicit. Algorithm~\ref{alg:long-short} opens a position
only on days when every long-side prediction is positive and every short-side
prediction is negative, and on the remaining days it holds the existing book
rather than moving to cash. Gate frequency is therefore a holding period, and
it is not a free parameter --- it falls out of the scale and centering of each
model's predictions. EXAMM's book turns over on $18.5\%$ of test days against
$51.7$--$94.3\%$ for every baseline, which is a mean holding period of roughly
five trading days against about one. A model is only graded on the horizon it
actually holds, so we report rank IC at $h{=}1$, the horizon the forecasting
literature reports, and at $h{=}3$ and $h{=}5$, the horizon this strategy
trades. Longer horizons are shown so that the reader can see where the effect
closes rather than only where it is largest.

Read that way the two tables are consistent. At $h{=}1$ EXAMM is mid-field:
iTransformer is ahead of it and PatchTST ties it, and no pairwise difference is
distinguishable from zero. From $h{=}2$ to $h{=}10$ EXAMM leads every baseline,
and by $h{=}20$ it has lost the lead again. The advantage is not uniform skill;
it is skill located at a particular horizon, and it is the horizon the book is
held. Two qualifications belong with that claim. First, only the gaps against
the weakest arms clear a bootstrap confidence interval at $n{=}12$ cells, and
DLinear --- $194$ parameters --- ties EXAMM at every horizon. Second, the size
of the horizon lift tracks parameter count across the eight models
($\rho = -0.786$), so what the evidence supports is that low-capacity models
retain the component of the cross-sectional signal that survives past one day,
not that neuroevolution discovered something unavailable to the other
architectures. That reading is consistent with the parameter-count and on-device results of Table~\ref{tab:param-counts} and Table~\ref{tab:ina219_results} rather than an additional claim on top of it.

\subsection{Respberry Pi Zero Results}
\label{sec:ina-219}
We evaluate each trained RNN on-device on a Raspberry Pi Zero to characterize inference accuracy, latency, and power consumption under realistic edge-deployment conditions. Each evaluation loads a serialized \texttt{RNN\_Genome} produced by EXAMM~\cite{examm} together with its saved normalization parameters (min--max or mean--standard-deviation), applies the same normalization to the held-out test series, and reports mean squared error (MSE) and mean absolute error (MAE) over the full test set. Wall-clock inference time is measured around the forward pass only (excluding I/O and prediction-writing overhead) using \texttt{std::chrono::high\_resolution\_clock}, from which we derive per-data-point latency and throughput (points/second). Concurrently with inference, an INA219 current/power sensor connected over I2C is polled from a dedicated sampling thread, recording bus voltage, shunt voltage, current, and power at fixed intervals; energy consumption is obtained by trapezoidal integration of power over the sampler's timestamped readings, and normalized by the number of test points to yield an energy-per-inference estimate. This setup allows us to directly relate model size and predictive accuracy to their on-device latency and energy cost.
