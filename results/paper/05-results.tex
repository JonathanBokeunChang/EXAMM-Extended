\subsection{Pooled vs.\ Alternative Constructions}

Table~\ref{tab:pooling-ablation} compares three dataset constructions on the Baseline Portfolio, reporting test-period rank IC, MSE, and the return of a simple long/short portfolio that holds the ten highest-ranked and shorts the ten lowest-ranked stocks. Under individual construction a separate model is trained on each stock's own series; under concatenated construction the fifty series are stacked side by side into one wide series, so a single model maps $300$ predictors to $50$ outputs and input dimensionality grows with the universe; under pooled construction one model is trained across all stocks with parameters shared over the cross-section, retaining the six-predictor, single-output shape of the individual construction. Pooling improves every metric in both the 2022 and 2023 folds and is the construction used for all models, including the baselines, in the experiments that follow.

The gain in MSE is modest, at $2.0\%$ in 2022 and $3.7\%$ in 2023, but the effect on cross-sectional ranking ability is substantially larger. Individual training produces near-zero rank IC in 2022 ($+0.0001$) and negative rank IC in 2023 ($-0.0050$), meaning its predictions carry little or no usable information about relative performance, whereas pooled training attains positive rank IC in both folds and reaches $+0.0195$ in 2023. Because the long/short strategy allocates on relative rather than absolute predictions, this difference in ranking quality, rather than the small MSE gap, drives the trading results: pooling raises the 2022 return from $+10.47\%$ to $+12.73\%$ and converts a $-4.17\%$ loss in 2023 into a $+30.24\%$ gain. Concatenated construction performs worst throughout, with negative rank IC in both folds and returns of $-28.48\%$ and $-17.03\%$. Stacking the cross-section into one wide series makes the entire universe a single training example per date: the fifty stocks then supply $3{,}290$ training sample-steps to a model that evolves to some $17{,}000$ edges, fewer than one observation per parameter, where pooling supplies $164{,}500$ sample-steps to roughly $66$. Adding stocks to a pooled panel adds examples and leaves the model the same size; adding them to a concatenated panel enlarges the model without adding a single example. Pooling keeps each series intact while sharing parameters across them, which is what allows the model to learn cross-sectional structure rather than a single averaged trajectory.

\subsection{EXAMM Hyperparameter Search}
\label{sec:examm-hparams}

Speciation is central to convergence behaviour in distributed neuroevolutionary search: the island population structure determines how much architectural diversity is maintained, while the repopulation interval controls how aggressively converged islands are reseeded from elsewhere in the population. Work by Lyu~\etal~finds tunning the repopulation strategy can be important for adopting to a new dataset\cite{lyu2021improving}\cite{lyu2023online}.  We therefore swept both parameters jointly, over island counts $\{10, 20, 40\}$ and repopulation intervals $\{250, 500, 1000\}$, evaluating all $9$ configurations on both test cohorts on baseline portfolio.

Table~\ref{tab:hp-grid} reports the long short trading results. The repopulation frequency of $500$ setting yields the strongest returns at every island count in both 2022 and 2023, whereas the number of islands setting differ across 2022 and 2023 test year.
Given that additional islands increase search cost without a corresponding gain, we use $10$ islands with repopulation frequency of $500$ for all subsequent experiments.

\subsection{Forecast Quality}
\label{sec:examm-vs-transformers}
Transformer models generally perform well on real-world, multidimensional data, yet simpler architectures such as linear models have been claimed to outperform them for time series forecasting~\cite{zeng2023transformers}. We bring these different families of models together to compare their ability to forecast stock returns and how that translates into portfolio returns. We tested the linear model DLinear~\cite{zeng2023transformers}, along with the Transformer-based PatchTST~\cite{nie2022time}, iTransformer~\cite{liu2024itransformer}, DeformTime~\cite{shu2024deformtime}, and TimeMixer~\cite{wang2023timemixer}, with an input length of $96$ and an output length of $1$. We chose an input length of $96$ because it is the most widely reported input sequence length across these models, and an output length of $1$ so that the results are directly comparable to the one-step-ahead forecasts of the EXAMM-evolved RNNs. All other hyperparameters were adopted from the default settings.

We additionally include two fixed-topology recurrent baselines, LSTM-i and GRU-i. Each is a two hidden layer recurrent network whose hidden layer size equals the input size. Models were trained with $1000$ backpropagation epochs with learning rate of $1e-4$. All experiments were run 10 times. 


Table~\ref{tab:ic} reports the average Pearson information coefficient of the raw return predictions. Results reported here is independent of the trading strategy. It shows the prediction performance comparasion among models. Values in bold are the highest IC for the trading year; underlined values are the second highest. And values marked with underscore are the second highest IC in that trading year. EXAMM attains the highest mean IC ($+0.0106$), followed by PatchTST ($+0.0069$) and DLinear ($+0.0067$); DeformTime is the only model with a negative mean IC ($-0.0019$). All values are small in absolute terms, which is expected for one-step-ahead daily return prediction, and no model is consistently positive across every panel-year cell: even EXAMM is negative in two of twelve cells.

\subsection{Portfolio Returns}

Table~\ref{tab:net-returns} reports the net returns of the 4 validation portfolios for the 2022, 2023, and 2024 trading years, using a simple daily long-short strategy that longs the 10 stocks with the highest predicted return and shorts the 10 stocks with the lowest predicted return. The table also includes the portfolio returns for Buy \& Hold (B\&H) and daily equal-weight (EW) strategies as benchmark results. EXAMM achieves the highest mean net return ($+11.02\%$), roughly $2.7\times$ that of the next-best model (TimeMixer, $+4.09\%$), and is the best or second-best model in seven of the twelve cells. Only three of the eight models exceed buy-and-hold on the mean ($+2.64\%$): EXAMM, TimeMixer, and DLinear. The remaining five, including three of the four Transformer variants, fall below a strategy that uses no predictions at all.

The two fixed-topology recurrent baselines, LSTM-i and GRU-i, return $-5.62\%$ and $-4.56\%$, respectively, placing them among the weakest columns in the study. This gap highlights the benefit of the evolved topology discovered through neuroevolution, rather than any advantage inherent to recurrent architectures with memory cells alone.

\subsection{Behaviour across market regimes}


Table~\ref{tab:net-returns-by-year} averages each column of Table~\ref{tab:net-returns} within trade years, and separates two effects that the pooled mean conflates. The 2022 test year is a drawdown regime across $V1$--$V4$, with buy-and-hold at $-10.05\%$ and equal-weight at $-17.39\%$; 2023 and 2024 are recovery regimes with $+11.18\%$ and $+6.80\%$ for buy-and-hold.

EXAMM is the highest-returning model in all three trade years, beating buy-and-hold by $25.51$ points in the drawdown year and by $1.72$ points in the 2024 recovery, and trailing it by only $2.11$ points in 2023, when every fixed architecture except DLinear and TimeMixer loses money. Averaged over the three years it returns $+11.02\%$, against $+2.64\%$ for buy-and-hold and $+4.09\%$ for TimeMixer. Table~\ref{tab:net-returns} shows why this average is stable: EXAMM is negative in $4$ of $12$ panel-years, but those losses are small ($-5.43\%$ to $-2.88\%$) while its gains are large ($+36.68\%$, $+35.22\%$, $+21.34\%$, $+17.19\%$), so the within-year averages remain clearly positive in every regime. For the transformer baselines, large per-panel gains are matched or outweighed by losses of similar size, leaving year averages near or below zero. EXAMM and TimeMixer are the only two columns positive in all three trade years, and EXAMM's three-year mean is larger by a factor of roughly $2.7$. We therefore characterise EXAMM's contribution as consistency across regimes rather than as outperformance in any one of them.

\subsection{Overall Comparison}
Table~\ref{tab:model-summary} averages four evaluation metrics over the validation portfolios, alongside each model's parameter count: information coefficient (IC), net return, Sharpe ratio, and maximum drawdown (MDD), the largest peak-to-trough decline in portfolio value. The benchmarks are listed with net return only, as buy-and-hold and equal weight issue no predictions and therefore have no IC, and their fixed allocations are not comparable on the risk metrics. EXAMM is the only model that is best on all four: IC ($+0.0106$ against $+0.0069$ for PatchTST), net return ($+11.02\%$ against $+4.09\%$ for TimeMixer), Sharpe ($+0.74$ against $+0.43$), and MDD ($-10.76\%$ against $-12.49\%$ for DLinear). Only three models return more than buy-and-hold's $+2.64\%$, and only EXAMM does so by a wide margin.

The parameter column shows that this does not come from capacity. EXAMM's evolved networks average $66$ weights, fewer than any other model in the comparison and two to four orders of magnitude below the Transformers ($16{,}836$ to $817{,}153$). Across the field, size and performance are unrelated or inversely related: iTransformer is the largest model at $817{,}153$ parameters and returns $-0.29\%$, DeformTime at $67{,}449$ parameters records the only negative IC in the table ($-0.0019$), while the two smallest models, EXAMM and DLinear, place in the top three on every metric. The table also separates prediction quality from tradeable profit: PatchTST has the second-highest IC ($+0.0069$) yet a net return of $-1.55\%$, so a model can rank the cross-section correctly on average and still lose money once positions are formed. EXAMM is the one model where accuracy, return, risk-adjusted return, and downside protection align, and it does so at the smallest model size. Figure~\ref{fig:horizon} shows where this comes from. EXAMM's rank IC rises from $+0.0068$
at a one-day horizon to $+0.0142$ at ten days, while every model above $300$ parameters
declines over the same range; at $14.5\%$ turnover its positions are held about seven
trading days, squarely within that band. Trading rarely is also cheaper: transaction costs
remove $0.9$ points from its gross return, against up to $3.4$ points for the
daily-rebalancing baselines.

Table~\ref{tab:examm-cost} reports the computational cost of EXAMM. EXAMM performs architecture search asynchronously in parallel: a master process maintains the population and distributes candidate genomes to worker processes, which train and evaluate them independently without global synchronisation barriers. Scaling is consequently near-linear in the number of workers. Total compute remains approximately constant at $8.3$--$9.9$ core-hours across all configurations tested, while wall-clock time decreases from $72.8$ minutes at $8$ ranks to $16.2$ minutes at $32$ ranks, the configuration used in all other experiments, and $3.9$ minutes at $128$ ranks. The search is CPU-only and requires no accelerator at any stage. At $8$ and $16$ ranks, core counts typical of contemporary laptop and desktop processors, a complete $10{,}000$-genome search completes in $72.8$ and $37.1$ minutes respectively, whereas the Transformer baselines require GPU training for models of up to $817{,}153$ parameters.

The resulting models are correspondingly inexpensive to deploy. Evolved genomes average $66$ parameters and execute in $10.82\,\mu$s per prediction on a Raspberry Pi Zero, a passively cooled single-core device, at $476.7$~mW above idle draw. Inference latency is therefore negligible relative to the daily rebalancing interval, and the sub-watt power requirement permits operation on battery or embedded hardware. The same networks that produce the returns in Table~\ref{tab:net-returns-by-year} can thus be both discovered and deployed on commodity hardware, which is not feasible for the baseline architectures.
