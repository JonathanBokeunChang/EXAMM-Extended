% The \emph{individual} construction fits a separate model per stock, so no information is shared across the universe. The \emph{concatenated} construction fits one model over all stocks by stacking their predictors into a wide series (300 inputs, 50 outputs), making input dimensionality scale with universe size. The \emph{pooled panel} adopted here retains the six-predictor, single-output structure of the individual construction while fitting one model across all stocks, so parameters are shared across the universe and input dimensionality remains fixed regardless of how many stocks are included.

Table~\ref{tab:pooling-ablation} compares pooled and individual training on the Baseline Portfolio, reporting test-period IC, MSE, and the return of a simple long/short portfolio that holds the ten highest-ranked and shorts the ten lowest-ranked stocks. Pooling improves every metric in both the 2022 and 2023 folds. The gain in MSE is modest, however the effect on cross-sectional ranking ability is substantially larger: individual training produces near-zero IC in 2022 and negative IC in 2023, meaning its predictions carry little or no usable signal about relative performance, whereas pooled training attains positive IC in both folds. Because the long/short strategy allocates on relative rather than absolute predictions, this difference in ranking quality, rather than the small MSE gap, drives the trading results. Pooling improves the long/short return in 2022 and, in 2023, converts a strategy that loses money under individual training into a profitable one. We therefore adopt pooled training for all subsequent experiments.

\subsection{EXAMM Hyperparameter Search}
\label{sec:examm-hparams}

Speciation is central to convergence behaviour in neuroevolutionary search: the island
population structure determines how much architectural diversity is maintained, while the
repopulation interval controls how aggressively converged islands are reseeded from elsewhere in
the population. Too frequent repopulation discards partially converged architectures before they
can be refined; too infrequent repopulation allows islands to stagnate. We therefore swept both
parameters jointly, over island counts $\{10, 20, 40\}$ and repopulation intervals
$\{250, 500, 1000\}$, evaluating all $9$ configurations on both test cohorts.

Table~\ref{tab:hp-grid} reports the results. The repopulation interval is the more consequential
of the two parameters: the $500$ setting yields the strongest returns at every island count in
both cohorts, whereas $250$ and $1000$ are inconsistent and in several configurations produce
losses. Island settings differ across 2022 and 2023 test year.
Given that additional islands increase search cost without a corresponding gain, we adopt
$(10~\text{islands}, 500)$ repopulation frequency for all subsequent experiments.

\subsection{Forecast Quality}
\label{sec:examm-vs-transformers}
Transformer models generally perform well on real-world, multidimensional data, yet simpler architectures such as linear models have been claimed to outperform them for time series forecasting~\cite{zeng2023transformers}. We bring these different families of models together to compare their ability to forecast stock returns and how that translates into portfolio returns. We tested the linear model DLinear~\cite{zeng2023transformers}, along with the Transformer-based PatchTST~\cite{nie2022time}, iTransformer~\cite{liu2024itransformer}, DeformTime~\cite{shu2024deformtime}, and TimeMixer~\cite{wang2023timemixer}, with an input length of $96$ and an output length of $1$. We chose an input length of $96$ because it is the most widely reported input sequence length across these models, and an output length of $1$ so that the results are directly comparable to the one-step-ahead forecasts of the EXAMM-evolved RNNs. All other hyperparameters were adopted from the default settings.

We additionally include two fixed-topology recurrent baselines, LSTM-i and GRU-i. Each is a two hidden layer recurrent network whose hidden layer size equals the input size.

Table~\ref{tab:ic} reports the Pearson information coefficient of the raw forecasts, measured before the entry rule is applied and before transaction costs, and so is the only quantity we report that is independent of the trading rule. EXAMM attains the highest mean IC ($+0.0106$), followed by PatchTST ($+0.0068$) and DLinear ($+0.0067$); DeformTime is the only model with a negative mean IC ($-0.0019$). All values are small in absolute terms, which is expected for one-step-ahead daily return prediction, and no model is consistently positive across every panel-year cell: even EXAMM is negative in two of twelve cells.

\subsection{Portfolio Returns}

Table~\ref{tab:net-returns} reports net return at the pre-committed L/S~10 book. EXAMM achieves the highest mean net return ($+11.02\%$), roughly $2.7\times$ the next-best model (TimeMixer, $+4.09\%$), and is the best or second-best model in seven of twelve cells. Only three of the eight models exceed buy-and-hold on the mean ($+2.64\%$): EXAMM, TimeMixer and DLinear. The remaining five, including three of the four Transformer variants, fall below a strategy that uses no predictions at all.

The two fixed-topology recurrent baselines are informative here. Despite sharing EXAMM's pipeline, normalisation and entry rule, LSTM-i and GRU-i return $-5.62\%$ and $-4.56\%$ respectively, placing them among the weakest columns in the study. The gap between them and EXAMM is therefore attributable to the evolved topology rather than to any component of the surrounding infrastructure.

\subsection{Behaviour across market regimes}
Table~\ref{tab:net-returns-by-year} averages each column within trade years, and separates two effects that the pooled mean conflates. The 2022 test year is a drawdown regime in all four panels, with buy-and-hold at $-10.05\%$ and equal-weight at $-17.39\%$; 2023 and 2024 are recovery regimes ($+11.18\%$ and $+6.80\%$ for buy-and-hold).

In 2022, \emph{all eight} models are positive while both ungated benchmarks are sharply negative. Because the entry rule of Algorithm~\ref{alg:long-short} is common to every model column and absent from both benchmark columns, we attribute this to reduced market exposure under the gate rather than to forecast skill in any individual model, and we caution against reading EXAMM's $+15.46\%$ in 2022 as evidence for the evolved architecture specifically. Consistent with this reading, EXAMM's excess over buy-and-hold is $+25.52$ points in 2022 but $-2.11$ and $+1.72$ points in 2023 and 2024, and over the full study it beats buy-and-hold in eight of twelve cells with all four losses occurring in recovery-regime cells.

What does separate the models is behaviour once the gate stops helping. In 2023 and 2024 only EXAMM ($+9.07\%$, $+8.52\%$) and TimeMixer ($+4.31\%$, $+6.22\%$) remain positive; the fixed RNNs lose between $9.7$ and $14.6$ points per year, and iTransformer, PatchTST and DeformTime all turn negative. EXAMM and TimeMixer are the only two columns positive in all three trade years, and EXAMM's margin is the larger by a factor of roughly $2.7$. We therefore characterise EXAMM's contribution as consistency across regimes rather than as outperformance in any one of them.

\subsection{Overall comparison}
Table~\ref{tab:model-summary} summarises all seven metrics per model. EXAMM ranks first on every one: IC ($+0.0106$), MSE, MAE, net return ($+11.02\%$), Sharpe ($+0.74$), maximum drawdown ($-10.76\%$ against a field mean of $-15.99\%$) and turnover. The drawdown result is the most consistent of these at the cell level: EXAMM records the shallowest drawdown in five of twelve cells and is best or second-best in eight, with a per-cell range of $-7.49\%$ to $-16.13\%$, so the mean is not driven by a small number of favourable cells. It achieves this with three to four orders of magnitude fewer parameters than the Transformer baselines.

Two of those seven columns deserve qualification rather than credit. EXAMM's lead on MSE and MAE is $0.5\%$ over DLinear, negligible beside the $17$-point spread in net return: point accuracy does not order these models, which is the same conclusion the pooling ablation of Table~\ref{tab:pooling-ablation} reaches on a different comparison. Turnover is not a performance metric at all but a description of the strategy --- EXAMM re-enters the book on $14.5\%$ of test days against $52$--$94\%$ for every baseline, and because positions are held rather than flattened on the remaining days, its mean holding period is roughly seven trading days against one. That difference is the mechanism behind both the transaction-cost advantage and the shallower drawdown, and it should be read as an explanation of the return column rather than as independent evidence for the evolved architecture.
